Crimes can be broken down into **elements**, *each of which* the prosecution must prove [beyond a reasonable doubt.](../ch1/) Criminal elements are set forth in criminal statutes, or cases in jurisdictions that allow for common-law crimes. With exceptions, every crime has at least three elements: (1) a criminal act, also called ***actus reus***; (2) a criminal intent, also called ***mens rea***; and (3) **concurrence** of the act and intent, meaning that the defendant had the required criminal intent to commit the act when they committed the criminal act. The term **conduct** is often used to reflect the concurrence of the elements of *actus reus* and *mens rea*.

> INFO: **Conduct**
>The Model Penal Code defines **conduct** as an "action or omission and its accompanying state of mind, or, where relevant, a series of acts and omissions."(1)
>{ .annotate }
>
>1. Model Penal Code [§ 1.13(5).](https://www.criminallawweb.net/mpc/mpc.html#1.13)

Recall from [Chapter 1](../ch1/1-5.md) that not all crimes require a bad result, such as harm. If a crime does require a bad result, the prosecution must also prove the additional elements of causation and harm.

> EXAMPLE: **Examples of Crimes That Do Not Require a Bad Result**
> **Example 1.** Conrad is driving 70 mph on a highway that has a 55 mph speed limit. This is an **infraction** or **violation**, as discussion in [Section 1.5.](../ch1/1-5.md/#infractions)
>
> **Example 2.** Janine gets into a fight with her boyfriend Conrad after the senior prom. She grabs Conrad’s car keys out of his hand, jumps into his car, and locks all the doors. When Conrad strides over to the car, she starts the engine, puts the car into drive, and tries to run him down. It is dark and difficult for Janine to see, so Conrad easily gets out of her way and is unharmed. However, Janine is thereafter arrested and charged with attempted murder.
> 
> In this case, the prosecution has to prove the elements of criminal act, criminal intent, and concurrence for attempted murder. The prosecution does not have to prove causation or that Conrad was harmed because attempt crimes, including attempted murder, do not have a bad result requirement. Attempt and other incomplete or inchoate crimes are discussed in [Chapter 8.](../ch8/index.md) 

Another requirement of some crimes is **attendant circumstances**. **Attendant circumstances** are specified factual circumstances or factors that must be present when the crime is committed. These could include the crime’s methodology, location or setting, and victim characteristics, among others.

> EXAMPLE: **Example of Attendant Circumstances**
> [Wis. Stat. § 943.32](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/943/iii/32/1) defines **Robbery** as taking property from the owner's person or presence with the intent to steal (a) by using force or (b) by threatening the imminent use of force.(1)
>{ .annotate }
>
>1. This is a Class E felony, allowing for punishment as a fine not to exceed $50,000 or imprisonment not to exceed 15 years, or both. [Wis. Stat. § 939.50(3)(e).](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/939/iv/50/3/e)
>
> Also, [the statute](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/943/iii/32) increases the seriousness of the punishment for robbery if committed by using or threatening to use a dangerous weapon.(1)
>{ .annotate }
>
>1. This is a Class C felony, allowing for punishment as a fine not to exceed $100,000 or imprisonment not to exceed 40 years, or both. [Wis. Stat. § 939.50(3)(c.](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/statutes/statutes/939/iv/50/3/c)
>
>To charge a defendant with the more serious class of robbery, the prosecution must the **attendant circumstance** of the defendant's use or threat to use a dangerous weapon in the course of the robbery.
